Understanding academic malpractice matters whether or not you ever use assignment help, because it defines the line between legitimate support and something that puts your qualification at risk. This page explains what Pearson's Centre Guide says about authenticity, how a concern is raised, what happens next, and how guidance you learn from differs from submitting someone else's work as your own. It follows Pearson's Centre Guide to BTEC internal assessment (version 4.1, August 2026). Pearson publishes separate guidance on malpractice sanctions, and your centre has its own policy, so check both.

What Counts as BTEC Academic Malpractice?

Pearson's rule is that evidence must be authentic: your own work, which can be judged fully against the criteria. You sign a declaration of authenticity when you submit, and the assessor signs a declaration too. In general terms, malpractice includes plagiarism (presenting someone else's work or ideas as your own without acknowledgement), collusion (unauthorised collaboration presented as individual work), fabrication (inventing data, sources or evidence) and contract cheating (submitting work someone else produced for you). Your centre's malpractice policy sets out the definitions it applies.

On artificial intelligence, the Centre Guide repeats the JCQ position. Students who misuse AI so that the work is not their own commit malpractice and may attract severe sanctions. If any section is reproduced directly from AI-generated responses, you must identify it, and it cannot show you met the criteria independently, so it will not be rewarded. See BTEC assignment submission rules.

How Is a Concern Raised?

Pearson says authentication is the responsibility of each centre. Your assessor must only accept authentic evidence, and where teachers doubt that work is a student's own they must investigate and take appropriate action. An assessor who doubts the work must not sign their own authenticity declaration. Centres commonly use plagiarism-detection software such as Turnitin, and tutors who know your usual standard may notice an unexplained change. The Centre Guide says centres should consider authenticity when setting assignments, for example by giving each student a different research focus to reduce copying or collusion.

If some or all of the evidence is found not to be authentic, the assessor takes appropriate action, including following the centre's malpractice policy and reporting suspected malpractice to Pearson as required. Pearson says centres must report all suspected cases of learner malpractice to it.

What Are the Consequences?

Pearson decides the sanctions for malpractice reported to it, and publishes them in its centre guidance for dealing with malpractice and maladministration, so check that guidance and your centre's policy for the sanctions that apply to your qualification. The Centre Guide also says that where work is found to be inauthentic, a learner may be given one resubmission and/or retake opportunity under its updated criteria. That is not automatic: the Lead Internal Verifier authorises it and the usual conditions apply. See our resubmission guide.

Pearson says students should be made aware of the importance of submitting their own work and the consequences of failing to do so, and that this should be detailed in your centre's own plagiarism and malpractice policy. If you are accused, ask your centre for its procedure in writing and use its appeals route. See how to appeal a BTEC grade.

How Learning Aids Differ From Contract Cheating

Pearson requires that work submitted for assessment is demonstrably your own. Guidance on how criteria work, worked examples you study, and structured frameworks are learning aids, and the key test is whether you understand the material and produce your own final answer. Submitting someone else's work as your own is different in kind, not only degree. Your centre's policy decides what support is acceptable on a specific assignment, so ask your tutor if you are unsure.

Responsible use treats any guidance as a study aid you learn from, in the same way as a tutorial or a worked example in a textbook, rather than a substitute for producing your own final answer.

What does responsible use of assignment help look like? You understand why each part of an answer meets a criterion, you write your own submission, and you sign the declaration of authenticity truthfully.

Our Position on Academic Integrity

Guidance, model answers and criteria-mapping are provided as learning aids. You are responsible for understanding and personally producing your final submission.

Is using BTEC assignment help considered cheating?

Pearson's rule is that submitted work must be demonstrably your own, and that misuse which makes it not your own is malpractice. Using guidance to learn how criteria work, then producing your own final answer, is different from submitting someone else's work. Your centre's policy decides what support is acceptable on a particular assignment, so ask your tutor if you are unsure.

What happens if I'm accused of plagiarism on a BTEC assignment?

Your centre follows its malpractice policy, and Pearson says centres must report suspected learner malpractice to it. Pearson decides the sanctions for cases reported to it. Ask your centre for its procedure in writing, and see our appeals page for the route if you believe procedures were not followed.

Can a BTEC malpractice finding affect my whole qualification?

It can. Sanctions are decided by Pearson for cases reported to it and are set out in its guidance for dealing with malpractice and maladministration. The Centre Guide also says that where work is inauthentic, one resubmission and/or retake may be authorised under its criteria. Check Pearson's guidance and your centre's policy for what applies to you.

Does Pearson use plagiarism-detection software?

Pearson's Centre Guide places responsibility for authentication on each centre, and many centres use tools such as Turnitin. Your centre's policy says which tools it uses. Pearson also says teachers who doubt that work is a student's own must investigate and take appropriate action.
